You need two combs. Matching ones are best. No batteries, no app, and please leave everyone's hair out of it.

Hold one comb in front of the other, with the teeth pointing the same way and about a finger's width between them. Look through both sets of teeth toward a light background. Slide one sideways. Then turn it a little.

Alongside the fine teeth, you should see broader bands of light and dark. They shift as you move the combs. A small turn can change the whole arrangement.

This is the comb version of the [Exploratorium's moiré demonstration](https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/moire-patterns). I like the equipment list. A bathroom drawer has most of a physics exhibit in it.

## Where the broad bands come from

Neither comb has those wide stripes cut into it. The stripes appear because of how the two sets of teeth overlap.

In one region, a tooth on the nearer comb sits in front of a tooth on the farther one. Their dark shapes cover much the same view, and the gaps beside them let light through. That region looks relatively light.

Elsewhere, the nearer teeth cover the farther comb's gaps. Less light gets through. That region looks dark.

With the combs slightly misaligned, the overlap changes across the teeth. The broad bands mark where the gaps line up well and where they do not. The Exploratorium describes moiré as a way to magnify differences between repetitive patterns. A tiny mismatch becomes something you can see across the room.

There is no extra striped object hiding between the combs. Separate them and the broad pattern disappears.

## Change one thing at a time

Try these in order, watching the broad bands rather than the individual teeth.

1. Keep the angle fixed and slide one comb sideways. Watch the bands travel.
2. Bring the combs close to parallel, then slowly turn one. Compare the width and spacing of the bands.
3. Set one comb aside. Hold the other near a mirror and look through its teeth at their reflection. Tip one end away from the glass.

That last version, also suggested by the Exploratorium, is especially pleasing. The mirror supplies the second pattern without requiring a second comb. You can change the alignment between an object and its own reflection.

Two layers of window screen work too. Rotate one against the other and the fine mesh produces much larger shapes. A single screen can even make a moiré pattern with its shadow on a sheet of white card behind it.

## Keep the teeth in view

The demonstration can model wave interference, but the comb explanation does not need waves canceling one another. Opaque teeth block some paths for light. Open gaps admit others. Keeping that distinction makes the effect easier to understand.

It also leaves a good little puzzle in your hands. The teeth stay straight. Their spacing stays fixed. Yet the broad pattern can sweep sideways when you barely move a comb.

I would leave the two combs together after the demonstration. Someone will pick them up, notice the stripes, and turn one a little to find out what happens next.
