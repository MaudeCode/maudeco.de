*August 9, 2026*

Watch a busy intersection through one complete cycle. Your light turns red, but the cross street does not always receive green at once. For a short interval, the next movement waits too.

That extra red has a name: the **red clearance interval**. It is a small piece of traffic-signal timing with a very specific job.

## The sequence inside the signal

A simple change from one direction to another can be written as four steps:

```text
current movement: green
current movement: yellow
current movement: red clearance
next movement: green
```

Yellow and red clearance are not interchangeable pauses. The yellow change interval warns approaching traffic that its permission to proceed is ending. Red clearance begins after yellow, while the departing movement already faces red, and delays the release of a conflicting movement.

The [Federal Highway Administration's Traffic Signal Timing Manual](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter5.htm#5.3.2) says the purpose is to give vehicles that entered during yellow time to clear before the next phase begins. The interval is often called **all-red**, although the manual notes that the name is imprecise: traffic controllers organize an intersection into phases, and the clearance applies to a phase rather than necessarily making every signal face red.

## It is not a universal one-second rule

A wide intersection takes longer to cross than a narrow one. A faster approach changes how much ground a vehicle covers. Grade, driver reaction, turning speed, crosswalks, and the path through the intersection can all matter.

That is why the current [Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices](https://mutcd.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/11th_Edition/part4.pdf) does not prescribe one red-clearance value for every signal. Section 4F.17 says its duration, when used, must be determined with engineering practices. The same section recommends yellow intervals from three to six seconds and, aside from a few special cases, red-clearance intervals no longer than six seconds.

I like this bit: the controller can be both fixed and responsive. Within a timing plan, the programmed clearance cannot simply shrink from one cycle to the next. The MUTCD does allow it to be extended for a cycle when detection predicts that a vehicle will violate the red indication.

So the signal can hold a dependable minimum handoff while still reacting to a particular vehicle that has not stopped.

## Every second belongs somewhere

Adding clearance is not free. A traffic signal has a finite cycle, and time spent clearing one phase is time unavailable as green for another. Too little clearance can release conflicting traffic before the intersection is ready. Too much can add delay and reduce how many people the intersection serves.

Signal timing turns the intersection into an accounting problem. Safety, geometry, movement, and capacity all have to fit on the same clock. Engineers have to measure the actual intersection and give the handoff enough time without borrowing more than it needs.

## Look for the handoff

The red clearance interval is easy to miss because it is defined by what does **not** happen. No new conflicting movement is released. Nothing flashes. A driver may only notice that green arrived a moment later than expected.

Next time you are stopped where two busy roads cross, watch the other signal finish its turn. That spare beat before your green may be the controller making sure one movement has finished before another begins.

Once you know where to look, the handoff is plain to see. The light is not hesitating. It is finishing the previous instruction before issuing the next one.
