Imagine getting to Z and still having one character left to recite.

For some nineteenth-century schoolchildren, that character was &. [Daniel Costa's history for Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ampersand) describes British students learning it after Z as a twenty-seventh member of the alphabet. The final item meant "and," which made saying the end of the list a little awkward.

The solution was to say more words. Those words eventually became the name of the character.

I like an etymology you can try with your mouth.

## Say it separately

The phrase was "and per se and." The Latin *per se* means "by itself." In effect, the speaker was saying that the character &, by itself, stood for the word "and."

[Merriam-Webster's account](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/the-history-of-ampersand) explains that this convention extended beyond the ampersand. A and I needed similar treatment because each could be a letter or a whole word. "I per se, I" distinguished the letter being named from its ordinary use in a sentence.

Say "and per se and" aloud. Then say it again without giving every part equal attention. You can hear how it could settle into *ampersand*. That is a useful way to understand the change, though a few repetitions at a desk are no substitute for the historical record.

The name preserves a pronunciation of an explanation. Nobody had to sit down and invent a suitably curly word for a curly mark.

There is a trap here, too. "The alphabet used to have twenty-seven letters" sounds like a universal rule that later changed. The more precise story is about a character included in alphabet teaching. It does not require imagining that every English speaker, everywhere, agreed on the same extra letter.

## The shape has an older story

The name came much later than the mark. The ampersand began as a *ligature*, a joining of letters, in this case the e and t of Latin *et*, meaning "and."

Britannica describes the different forms the character has taken and how its appearance varies with the typeface. Some make the joined letters easier to recognize than others. Once you know to look for *et*, the symbol becomes a small lettering puzzle rather than an arbitrary knot.

There is another clue in old-fashioned *&c*, which Britannica notes as an alternative to *etc.* Read the & as *et* and the abbreviation looks much less peculiar.

So the symbol and its name took different routes. Handwriting joined two letters. Recitation joined several spoken words. Both ended up in the same character on a keyboard.

Now the end of that old alphabet is the part I want to hear. After the familiar letters, one last spoken explanation, gradually becoming a word of its own.
