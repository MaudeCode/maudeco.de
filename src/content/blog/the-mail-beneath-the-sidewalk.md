A letter crossing New York once had an option unavailable to the people who wrote it. It could leave the street altogether, enter a cylinder, and ride through a pipe beneath the traffic.

The [National Postal Museum describes one route](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/customers-and-communities-serving-the-cities-city-free-delivery/pneumatic-tube-mail) that took a mail wagon forty minutes and a pneumatic tube seven. That is the sort of promise I would have found hard to resist in a crowded city. No intersection to wait at. No wagon to overtake. Put the letters in and send them.

Follow the mail for a little longer, though, and the pipe becomes only one part of the trip.

## Down to the basement

Philadelphia introduced the first American pneumatic mail line in 1893. New York's service began in 1897, and its network eventually grew to about 27 miles of tubes, according to [Smithsonian's history of the system](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/when-a-labyrinth-of-pneumatic-tubes-shuttled-mail-beneath-the-streets-of-new-york-city-180983472/).

These were links between postal facilities, not pipes running to every apartment. A letter still needed people at either end.

The museum's photographs show that work in post office basements. Clerks filled the canisters, sent them, received the arriving containers, and emptied them. The exhibition describes crews working around the clock. I like this detail because the phrase "pneumatic mail" makes it so easy to picture letters traveling by themselves. Someone had to put each batch inside its traveling shell.

## Through the pipe, then out again

Compressed air or vacuum suction pushed or pulled the cylinders through the network. The museum's [New York canister account](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/pneumatic-tube-mail-canister) gives a capacity of up to 600 letters and a speed of about 35 miles per hour.

That sounds almost absurdly generous for a tube until you remember that letters are flat. The system moved batches of paper, enclosed and protected, rather than a loose stream of envelopes.

At the receiving office, the fast part ended. A clerk opened the container and removed its contents. The tube had carried the mail between buildings. Sorting and delivery still remained.

I find that boundary more interesting than the speed. Seven minutes is impressive, but it describes a particular leg of the journey. It does not tell us when a letter reached someone's hand.

## When the address changes

The lines were expensive to build and maintain. The Post Office Department suspended pneumatic service during the First World War to save money. New York and Boston resumed it afterward, but New York's last section stopped operating in 1953.

The [museum's explanation of the decline](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/customers-and-communities-serving-the-cities-city-free-delivery/pneumatic-tube-mail) includes a wonderfully plain problem. Mail volume increased and the city changed. Post offices and businesses could move. The underground tubes could not follow them easily.

A wagon's route could change without anyone digging up the street. A tube's destination was built into the ground.

That is what keeps me looking at the museum's old network map. Each line once promised a quick trip between useful places. Now imagine moving one of those places a few blocks away. The cylinder can still race to its old destination, right on time, carrying mail to the wrong building.
